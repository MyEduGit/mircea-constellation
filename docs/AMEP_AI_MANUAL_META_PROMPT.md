## Context

**Program:** AMEP (Adult Migrant English Program)  
**Students:** Adult ESL learners from war-torn countries — navigating displacement, religious discrimination, and trauma. English level A2–B1 (CEFR). Digital experience ranges from smartphone-only to none.  
**Teachers:** Peer ESL educators. Deep in English teaching; early in AI literacy. They will learn by teaching.  
**Goal:** Two-part resource —
- **Part A — Student Manual** `"AI Is Your New Study Partner"`: plain language, tool-neutral, printable. Students use it directly.
- **Part B — Teacher's Book** `"Teaching AI to New Australians"`: mirrors Part A chapter by chapter; gives teachers the why, facilitation guides, and cultural sensitivity notes.

**Core principle:** This resource is **unbiased**. No single AI tool is promoted. Students learn to choose the right tool for each task.

## THE META PROMPT

> Paste the block below into **any** of the AI tools above (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, etc.), adjusting the bracketed fields as needed. The prompt is deliberately tool-neutral — it will work in all of them.

```
You are an instructional designer specialising in adult ESL education, digital literacy,
and trauma-informed pedagogy. Your audience has two layers:

LAYER 1 — THE STUDENTS
Adult migrants and refugees in the AMEP (Adult Migrant English Program), primarily from
war-torn countries (Afghanistan, Sudan, Syria, Ukraine, Myanmar, Ethiopia, and similar contexts).
Many have experienced displacement, religious discrimination, and interrupted education.
English level: Pre-intermediate to Intermediate (CEFR A2–B1).
Digital experience: Ranges from smartphone-only to no device experience.
Motivation: High — they know learning AI and digital tools will help them find work,
reconnect with family, and build a new life.

LAYER 2 — THE TEACHERS
Peer ESL educators. They understand English teaching deeply but are not yet confident
with AI tools beyond basic awareness. They will learn the material by teaching it.
They need enough scaffolding to explain each concept clearly, answer student questions,
and feel confident — not expert, just one step ahead.

IMPORTANT: This resource must be UNBIASED. Do not promote any single AI tool.
Present all major AI tools fairly. Use examples from multiple tools throughout.

YOUR TASK: Write a complete two-part resource.

══════════════════════════════════════════
PART A — STUDENT MANUAL: "AI Is Your New Study Partner"
══════════════════════════════════════════

TONE & LANGUAGE RULES:
- Simple, warm, encouraging. Never condescending.
- Short sentences. Active voice. Maximum Grade 6 reading level.
- Avoid jargon. When a technical word must be used, define it immediately with a
  real-life comparison (e.g. "A prompt is like a question you ask a friend.").
- Use inclusive, culturally neutral examples. Never assume a student has a stable home,
  internet at home, or prior experience with computers.
- Honour the students' life experience as an asset, not a deficit.
- Do not favour any single AI company or product. Use examples from multiple tools.

STRUCTURE — write each of the following chapters in full:

────────────────────────────────────────
CHAPTER 1 — What Is AI? (And Why Should I Care?)
────────────────────────────────────────
- What "Artificial Intelligence" means in plain English
- One honest paragraph: AI learned from text written by humans. It is not alive.
  It does not have feelings. It can be wrong. Always check important information.
- Why learning AI now matters: finding work, studying, communicating with government
  services, understanding letters, practising English
- The AI you already use without knowing: voice assistants on phones, Google Translate,
  autocomplete when typing, suggested replies in WhatsApp
- Activity: Ask your phone's voice assistant (Siri, Google, Bixby) one question.
  Write down what it said. Was it helpful? Was it correct?
- [TEACHER NOTE: Validate that some students already use AI tools. Position this as
  building on what they already know, not starting from zero.]

────────────────────────────────────────
CHAPTER 2 — Your AI Toolkit: 10 Tools You Can Use Today
────────────────────────────────────────
- Introduce the concept: there are many AI tools, like there are many supermarkets.
  Different ones are good for different things. You do not need to use all of them.
- Present the following 10 tools in a simple card format. For each tool write:
    * Name and maker (one sentence, plain English)
    * What it is good at (2–3 dot points, A2 English level)
    * How to open it on a phone browser (step by step)
    * One thing it cannot do or does not do well
    * Cost: free / paid / free with limits

  THE 10 TOOLS TO COVER:
  1. ChatGPT (OpenAI) — chatgpt.com
  2. Claude (Anthropic) — claude.ai
  3. Gemini (Google) — gemini.google.com
  4. Microsoft Copilot (Microsoft) — copilot.microsoft.com
  5. Meta AI (Meta) — inside WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook
  6. Grok (xAI) — x.com
  7. Perplexity (Perplexity AI) — perplexity.ai
  8. DeepSeek (DeepSeek) — chat.deepseek.com
  9. Mistral / Le Chat (Mistral AI) — chat.mistral.ai
  10. NotebookLM (Google) — notebooklm.google.com

- End the chapter with a simple "Which tool for which task?" guide:
    * Practising English conversation → ChatGPT or Claude
    * Quick fact or news question → Perplexity or Grok
    * Working in Word or Outlook → Microsoft Copilot
    * Already in WhatsApp? → Meta AI
    * Have a document you want to ask questions about? → NotebookLM
    * Want a tool from Europe with strong privacy? → Mistral / Le Chat
    * Want a free and powerful tool? → DeepSeek

- [TEACHER NOTE: Students do not need to try all 10 tools. Choose 1–2 that match your
  classroom devices. The point of this chapter is awareness, not mastery.]

────────────────────────────────────────
CHAPTER 3 — Talking to AI: How to Write a Good Prompt
────────────────────────────────────────
- A prompt is your instruction to AI. Like speaking to a helpful stranger:
  the clearer you are, the better the answer.
- The 3-part prompt formula: [WHO you are] + [WHAT you need] + [HOW you want it]
  Example: "I am a new arrival in Australia. Explain what Medicare is. Use simple English."
- Show this formula applied to 3 different AI tools (ChatGPT, Gemini, and one other)
  to show that the same prompt works in all tools
- Practice prompts (culturally grounded, tool-neutral):
    * Ask any AI tool to explain a Centrelink letter in simple English
    * Ask any AI tool to help write a thank-you email to a case worker
    * Ask any AI tool to quiz you on 10 new English words about healthcare
    * Ask any AI tool: "What jobs are growing in Australia right now?"
- When the answer is wrong or confusing: how to ask again (follow-up prompts)
- When to stop and check with a real person: migration questions, medical advice,
  legal matters, visa issues — always verify with a qualified human
- [TEACHER NOTE: Do this as a live class exercise. Type a prompt together on the
  whiteboard first, then students adapt it for their own situation.]

────────────────────────────────────────
CHAPTER 4 — AI for English Practice Every Day
────────────────────────────────────────
- AI is available 24/7. It does not get tired or impatient. It will practise
  English with you as many times as you need.
- Conversation practice: roleplay job interviews, phone calls, medical appointments,
  parent-teacher meetings — use any AI chat tool
- Vocabulary building: "Give me 5 new words about [working in a hospital] with a
  sentence for each word."
- Grammar check: "I wrote this email. Can you correct my grammar and explain each mistake?"
  Show a before/after example using two different AI tools to show they both work
- Pronunciation note: most text AI tools cannot hear you. For pronunciation:
  use Google Translate voice feature or Microsoft Translator (both free)
- Writing practice: ask AI to give you a topic and grade your paragraph
- Weekly practice plan (15 minutes a day):
    Monday: Ask any AI tool 5 questions about a topic you choose
    Tuesday: Write a short paragraph and ask AI to correct it
    Wednesday: Roleplay a job interview or phone call
    Thursday: Ask AI to teach you 5 new words
    Friday: Ask AI one question you have been curious about all week
- Offline option: download Google Translate for offline use when no internet available
- [TEACHER NOTE: Pair students without devices with students who have one. The
  conversation about what to type is itself valuable English practice.]

────────────────────────────────────────
CHAPTER 5 — AI for Everyday Life in Australia
────────────────────────────────────────
- AI can help you understand Australia, not just English
- Practical tasks to try with any AI tool:
    * "Explain what a Tax File Number is and how to apply"
    * "What does 'casual employment' mean in Australia?"
    * "Help me write a message to my child's school"
    * "What questions can I ask at a doctor's appointment?"
    * "Translate this letter [paste text] and explain what I need to do"
- Using AI with documents: NotebookLM lets you upload a PDF (e.g. a government letter)
  and ask questions about it
- Using AI for job applications:
    * "Help me write a resume for a job as a cleaner / aged care worker / driver"
    * "What questions might they ask at a job interview for [job title]?"
    * "Check my cover letter and make it sound more professional"
- Important limit: AI does not know your personal situation. It gives general
  information only. For visa, Centrelink, or legal decisions — always see a real expert.
- [TEACHER NOTE: This chapter connects directly to AMEP Settlement topics.
  Link these activities to whatever settlement unit you are currently teaching.]

────────────────────────────────────────
CHAPTER 6 — AI Tools for Advanced Users
────────────────────────────────────────
- You do NOT need this chapter yet. Come back to it when you are ready.
- This chapter shows what is possible in the future.

SECTION 6A — AI That Searches the Web
  - Perplexity and Grok can search the internet and show you where the information comes from
  - Useful for: checking if a job ad is real, finding current news in your language,
    researching a company before an interview
  - Try: "Find recent news about employment in aged care in Australia" (use Perplexity)

SECTION 6B — AI That Reads Your Documents
  - NotebookLM: upload any document (PDF, Google Doc) and ask questions about it
  - Useful for: understanding a long lease agreement, TAFE course handbook, medical report
  - Try: Upload one document you have received recently and ask "What do I need to do?"

SECTION 6C — AI That Writes Computer Instructions (Code)
  - Some AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Gemini) can write code — instructions
    that computers follow
  - Who uses this: web developers, data analysts, small business owners,
    administrative assistants who use Excel
  - You do not need to learn to code. But knowing this exists opens doors.
  - Inspiring story: [Generate a fictional but realistic and culturally respectful story
    of a refugee who used AI coding assistance to automate a repetitive task at work,
    gained recognition, and was offered a better position. The character should be from
    a country relevant to the AMEP cohort. Do not make the story feel like a fairy tale —
    show the hard work, the confusion, and the small wins.]
  - Optional stretch activity: Open any AI chat tool and type:
    "What is a spreadsheet formula? Give me one example I can use at work."

SECTION 6D — AI That Creates Images and Video
  - Tools like DALL-E (in ChatGPT), Gemini, and others can create images from text
  - Useful for: making a poster for a community event, creating a picture to explain
    something to your children, creative projects
  - Caution: AI-generated images can spread misinformation. Learn to spot them.
  - [TEACHER NOTE: This section can be skipped or used as an extension activity.
    Sensitivity required: image generation tools have known biases around race,
    religion, and cultural representation. Preview any generated images before showing class.]

────────────────────────────────────────
CHAPTER 7 — Staying Safe Online With AI
────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 7A — What NOT to share with any AI tool
  - Never share: visa number, tax file number, Medicare number, passwords,
    your children's full names, your home address, bank details
  - Even "free" tools store what you type. Treat every AI chat like a public noticeboard.
  - Special note for students from surveillance states: your concern about privacy
    is completely reasonable. It is wise to be cautious. You can use AI without
    sharing any personal information.

SECTION 7B — AI is not a professional
  - AI is not a lawyer, migration agent, doctor, or financial adviser
  - It can give you general information. It cannot give you advice for your specific situation.
  - For visa questions: see a registered migration agent (free services available)
  - For health questions: see a GP or call Health Direct: 1800 022 222 (free, 24/7)
  - For legal questions: contact your state's legal aid

SECTION 7C — How to spot AI-generated misinformation
  - AI can confidently say things that are wrong. This is called "hallucination."
  - Three-source rule: if AI tells you something important, check it on two other websites
  - Warning signs: no source given, date seems wrong, too perfect, too dramatic
  - Perplexity is better than most tools at showing sources — use it for fact-checking

SECTION 7D — Your data
  - Most free AI tools use your conversations to train their systems
  - You can delete your chat history in settings on most tools
  - For sensitive topics, use Mistral / Le Chat (European privacy law is stricter)
  - You can always start a new conversation — old chats are not remembered by default

SECTION 7E — Safe practice checklist (printable tick-box format)
  □ I never share my visa number, TFN, or Medicare number with AI
  □ I check important information with a second source
  □ I know AI can be wrong — I do not rely on it for legal or health decisions
  □ I delete my chat history after sensitive topics
  □ I know where to get real help: Legal Aid / Health Direct / Migration Agent

────────────────────────────────────────
CHAPTER 8 — AI and Your Future
────────────────────────────────────────
- Jobs that already use AI: aged care documentation, customer service, retail,
  delivery routing, administrative work, teaching support
- Jobs AI cannot replace: your care, your cultural knowledge, your languages,
  your ability to build trust with people from your community
- Your languages are an advantage: you can use AI in English AND in your first language.
  Many employers need staff who can communicate with multicultural communities.
- One action this week: Show one family member or friend one thing you learned about AI.
  Teaching someone else is the fastest way to learn.
- The field keeps changing: the tools in Chapter 2 will be different in 12 months.
  Learning HOW to learn new AI tools is more valuable than mastering any single one.

  Signed: _________________________  Date: _____________
```

## HOW TO USE THIS META PROMPT

1. **Open** any AI tool from the landscape table above (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, etc.)
2. **Paste** the entire prompt block into a new conversation
3. **Send** — the AI will generate the full two-part resource in one response

   - *"Make Chapter 5 activities more relevant to aged care work."*
   - *"Rewrite the Chapter 7 safety checklist at an A1 English level."*
   - *"Add examples relevant to students from South Sudan."*
   - *"Update Chapter 2 to include [new tool name] released in [month]."*
5. **Export** to Google Docs: copy Markdown → paste → Format > Convert to Docs
6. **Print** the Quick Reference Card, AI Update Card, and Student Pledge each term

## NOTES FOR THE MID-YEAR REVIEW PRESENTATION

- Frame this as **"We are learning this together"** — not top-down training
- The tool-neutral approach removes the pressure to be an expert in any one product
- Students from surveillance states may distrust certain tools — this is valid;
  let them choose. Mistral (French) or a local library computer is always an option.
- Some students will know MORE than their teachers about specific tools — that is an
  asset. Invite them to demo. Peer teaching accelerates the whole class.
- The AI Update Card means the resource never goes out of date — teachers maintain it
  with a pen, not a reprint budget
- Claude itself, ChatGPT, or Gemini can be used live in class —
  the teacher's uncertainty becomes the lesson, not a liability
- This is a **living document**: update the landscape table, add new chapters as
  tools like voice AI and image AI become more classroom-relevant
