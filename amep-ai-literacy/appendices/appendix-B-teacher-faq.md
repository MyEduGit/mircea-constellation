# Teacher's Book — Appendix B
## FAQ for Teachers

*Answers to the questions teachers most commonly ask — and the ones students ask
when teachers least expect them.*

---

### Section 1: Integrity and Academic Honesty

---

**Q: What if a student uses AI to cheat on a writing task?**

> **Short answer:** Redesign the task so that AI assistance becomes visible and discussable, rather than hidden.
>
> **Longer answer:**
> AI-generated text is increasingly difficult to detect with certainty. Tools that claim to "detect AI writing" produce significant false positives and should not be used to accuse students.
>
> More effective strategies:
> - Build in a verbal component: students explain their writing in a brief one-on-one conversation. If they cannot discuss it, the writing is not theirs.
> - Make AI use declared and visible: "You may use Claude to help draft. Submit the Claude conversation alongside your writing." This transforms the activity.
> - Design tasks around personal experience: "Describe a moment from your own life when..." — AI cannot answer this authentically.
> - Focus on process, not just product: collect drafts, pre-writing notes, and revisions. AI-generated text typically arrives complete with no drafting trail.
>
> The deeper question for AMEP: if a student uses AI to produce a draft and then edits it, reads it, and can discuss it — have they learned? In many cases, yes. The goal of this curriculum is to teach AI as a scaffold, not a crutch. Module 10 addresses academic integrity directly with students.

---

**Q: What if a student submits AI-generated text as their own work for assessment?**

> **Short answer:** Have a conversation with the student first. Understand the context before making a judgment.
>
> **Longer answer:**
> An AMEP student submitting AI-generated text may be:
> - Unaware that this is a concern (especially if AI use was not addressed in class).
> - Overwhelmed and using AI as a survival tool, not an attempt to deceive.
> - Unclear on the difference between using AI as a helper vs. submitting its output as their own.
>
> Recommended approach:
> 1. Have a private, non-accusatory conversation: "Can you tell me about how you wrote this?"
> 2. If AI was used: treat it as a teachable moment about academic integrity.
> 3. Give the student the opportunity to redo the task with support.
> 4. Document the conversation per your RTO's policies.
>
> Escalation to formal misconduct processes should only occur for deliberate, repeated, and clearly dishonest use — not for a learner who misunderstood the rules.

---

**Q: A student asked Claude to write their AMEP essay. Is that plagiarism?**

> Strictly speaking, in most Australian education contexts, submitting AI-generated work as your own without disclosure meets the definition of academic misconduct.
>
> In practice, apply the guidance above: context, conversation, and support before escalation.
>
> Going forward: teach students that Claude is a *grammar checker and co-editor*, not a *ghostwriter*.
> The distinction is: "Claude helped me check my work" vs. "Claude wrote my work."
> Module 10 makes this distinction explicit.

### Section 2: Privacy and Legal Questions

---

**Q: What does Australian privacy law say about students using AI tools?**

> ⏱ *This area is evolving rapidly. Verify current status at oaic.gov.au before advising students.*
>
> The *Privacy Act 1988* (Cth) governs how Australian organisations collect and handle personal information. Key points:
>
> - Anthropic (the company that makes Claude) is a US company. When Australian students use Claude, their data is processed on US servers.
> - Anthropic's privacy policy governs how conversation data is handled. Students with a free account should read this before sharing sensitive information. ⏱
> - Students can opt out of having their conversations used for training (check account settings). ⏱
> - Australia's government is developing an AI Governance Framework — check digital.gov.au for current status. ⏱
>
> **For classroom purposes:**
> The safeguard taught in this curriculum — never type personal information into AI — is the most effective practical protection, regardless of which laws apply.

---

**Q: A student is worried that Claude is listening through their phone camera or microphone. How do I respond?**

> This is a specific, answerable concern. Be precise.
>
> Claude (via the web interface or app) can access the microphone **only** when:
> - The student is actively using voice input mode.
> - The student explicitly tapped the microphone icon.
>
> Claude cannot:
> - Activate the microphone in the background.
> - Access the camera without explicit permission.
> - Access other apps on the phone.
> - Read files on the phone without the student uploading them.
>
> Say: "Claude only listens when you tell it to. When you are not using voice mode, it cannot hear you."
>
> If a student is concerned about an AI they did not choose to install, that is a different concern — and may be worth investigating on their specific device.

---

**Q: What are my obligations as a teacher if a student shares personal information with Claude in class?**

> Your duty of care obligations relate to the student's welfare — not to AI data handling.
>
> If a student accidentally shares personal information with Claude:
> 1. Help them close the conversation (start a new chat — this removes the history from the current session).
> 2. Reassure them: one conversation containing a name or address is low risk. The concern is habitually sharing sensitive documents.
> 3. Reinforce the safety rules without shaming.
>
> If the information shared relates to a child protection concern (e.g., a student describing a child's situation in detail), your mandatory reporting obligations apply as they would for any class disclosure.

### Section 3: Parent and Community Questions

---

**Q: What if a parent or family member objects to their adult child using AI tools in class?**

> AMEP students are adults. They have the right to make their own decisions about which tools they use.
>
> If a family member expresses concern, the recommended approach:
> 1. Take the concern seriously. Privacy and technology concerns often come from genuine experience.
> 2. Explain what the course teaches: how to use AI safely, how to protect privacy, how to check AI output.
> 3. Explain that students can always choose the paper alternative — no activity requires AI use.
> 4. Offer to meet with the family member to discuss the course.
>
> You are not required to change your curriculum because of a family preference, but building community trust is important in the AMEP context.

---

**Q: A student's cultural or religious background makes them uncomfortable with certain types of AI use (e.g., voice mode, photography). How do I handle this?**

> Always offer a paper alternative. This curriculum requires no student to speak aloud, appear on camera, or share any personal information with AI.
>
> Voice mode (Module 3) can be completed by typing. The paper pronunciation activity achieves the same skill. Attendance at the class is not conditional on AI use.
>
> If a student expresses a specific concern, ask what they would be comfortable with and design around that. Do not ask students to explain or justify their preferences.

### Section 4: Technical and Practical Questions

---

**Q: Claude's interface has changed since this book was printed. What do I do?**

> AI tools update frequently. ⏱ The *concepts* in this curriculum are stable; the *screenshots* are not.
>
> When the interface changes:
> 1. Walk students through the updated interface at the start of the lesson.
> 2. Frame it positively: "Claude has updated. Let us find the button together."
> 3. Note the change in your lesson notes so future teachers benefit.
>
> Teach students to adapt to interface changes as a skill — not as a failure of the curriculum.

---

**Q: Some of my students have very slow internet or expensive mobile data. How do I manage this?**

> Every module includes a paper alternative. No module requires all students to be online simultaneously.
>
> Practical strategies:
> - Demo on your device and projector. Students observe without using data.
> - Students who have data: complete the live activity.
> - Students who do not: complete the paper version and discuss with a partner.
> - Consider: is there classroom Wi-Fi? If so, use it to reduce data costs.
> - For homework: never require AI use. The paper alternative is always equal in learning value.

---

**Q: What if a student has no device at all?**

> AMEP classes sometimes include students with no personal device and no household access.
>
> Options:
> - Pair with a student who has a device — one device, two learners. All activities can be shared.
> - Use a shared classroom laptop or tablet for that student during class activities.
> - Provide printed materials for all paper-based activities.
> - For homework: the paper alternative is designed to be equivalent.
>
> No student should feel excluded from learning because they lack a device. The digital skills are built through observation, discussion, and paper practice as well as hands-on use.
