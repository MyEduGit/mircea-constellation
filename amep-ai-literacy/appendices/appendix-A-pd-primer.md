# Teacher's Book — Appendix A
## AI in 20 Minutes: A PD Primer for the Staff Room

*For AMEP teachers who want a quick, honest overview of AI before teaching this course.*
*Reading time: approximately 20 minutes.*

---

## What is AI, really?

**The honest version:**

Large language models (LLMs) — the technology behind Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini —
are trained on enormous amounts of human-written text.
During training, the model learns statistical patterns:
"When humans write [X], they tend to follow it with [Y]."

When you type a question, the model predicts the most statistically likely next words —
over and over — until it produces a response that looks like a thoughtful answer.

It is not thinking. It is not reasoning. It is not searching a database.
It is pattern completion at enormous scale.

**Why does this matter for teachers?**

Because it explains everything that AI does well *and* poorly:
- It does well when asked for common, well-represented patterns (grammar rules, polite emails, simple explanations).
- It fails when asked for facts that require current data, local knowledge, or careful verification.
- It "hallucinates" because prediction can produce a confident, fluent answer that is simply wrong.

## The five most important things to know before teaching this course

**1. You do not need to be an expert.**
You need to know enough to demo the tool and anticipate student questions.
This appendix covers that.

**2. Claude is not Google.**
Google finds websites. Claude writes answers.
Both can be wrong. Both need verification.
Claude is more useful for language tasks (drafting, explaining, correcting).
Google is more useful for finding official documents.

**3. The knowledge cutoff is real.**
Claude does not know what happened after its training cutoff date. ⏱
For anything current (news, timetables, opening hours), students should go to the source.

**4. Privacy is the non-negotiable.**
Personal information typed into Claude may be stored and used for AI training
(depending on account settings and Anthropic's current policies ⏱).
The classroom rule — no real names, addresses, passport numbers — is not paranoia.
It is good digital hygiene.

**5. AI will make mistakes in your classroom.**
This is a feature, not a bug.
Every time Claude gets something wrong in class, that is a teachable moment about verification.
Do not apologise for it. Use it.

## Quick orientation to the main AI tools

### Claude (claude.ai) — the primary tool in this curriculum

- Made by Anthropic (US company focused on AI safety).
- Free tier: available without account for basic queries; account required for history. ⏱
- Strengths: nuanced language, careful explanations, safety-focused.
- Weaknesses: not always up to date; can be verbose.
- Key features covered in this course: chat, voice mode, web search, Chrome extension.

### ChatGPT (chatgpt.com) — the most widely known competitor

- Made by OpenAI.
- Free tier available. ⏱
- Similar capabilities to Claude for most classroom tasks.
- Students may arrive already using ChatGPT. The skills from this course transfer directly.

### Gemini (gemini.google.com) — Google's AI tool

- Made by Google.
- Integrated with Google services (Gmail, Docs, Drive). ⏱
- Useful for students in the Google ecosystem.
- Skills transfer from this course.

### NotebookLM (notebooklm.google.com) — for document analysis

- Allows users to upload documents and ask questions about them.
- Good for students who receive long documents (tenancy agreements, health guides).
- More privacy-aware than general chat — only reads what you upload.

## What is hallucination? (A quick explainer for your next staff meeting)

A hallucination is when an AI states something confidently that is not true.

**Example:** Ask Claude what time a specific local pharmacy closes.
Claude may give a precise-sounding answer: "The pharmacy closes at 6:30 pm Monday to Friday."
But Claude may have invented this — or remembered a detail from a training document that is years old.

**Why does it happen?**
The model predicts the most plausible-sounding next words.
"A pharmacy closes at" is almost always followed by a time.
So it generates one — even if it has no reliable data for this specific pharmacy.

**How do you teach around it?**
You do not teach around it. You teach through it.
Every module ends with "AI can be wrong. How do we check?"
By Module 6, students are checking sources as a habit.

## Ethical landscape (brief)

**Bias:** AI reflects the biases in its training data. This is documented. Be alert to it.

**Copyright:** Text and images generated by AI may reproduce copyrighted content.
For classroom use (not publication), this is low-risk.

**Academic integrity:** AI-generated text submitted as a student's own work raises integrity questions.
This course teaches students to use AI as a tool, not a ghostwriter.
Module 10 addresses this directly.

**Employment:** AI is changing many industries. AMEP students often work in sectors
being affected (administration, customer service, data entry).
This is an opportunity to discuss upskilling — not to generate anxiety.

**Privacy law:** ⏱ Australia's Privacy Act 1988 applies to AI services handling personal data.
The government is developing an AI ethics framework. Current status: oaic.gov.au.

## What to say when a colleague asks "Is AI safe?"

> "It is a useful tool when used carefully.
> The same way we teach students to evaluate a website before trusting it,
> we teach them to evaluate AI output.
> The skills we are building — critical reading, source checking, privacy awareness —
> are the same skills we have always valued.
> AI makes them more urgent, not different."

## A 10-minute demo you can run at your next staff meeting

1. Open claude.ai on your laptop (no account needed for a basic demo).
2. Type: **"What is a simple way to explain AI to a beginner?"**
3. Read the reply aloud.
4. Then type: **"Now explain it in 3 sentences for someone with beginner English."**
5. Compare the two replies.
6. Say: "This is what we teach students to do — adjust the question to get what they need."
7. Then type something that will likely hallucinate: **"What time does [a specific local business] open?"**
8. Point out the confident but unverifiable answer.
9. Say: "This is why we teach verification."

Total time: 8–10 minutes. Powerful demonstration.
