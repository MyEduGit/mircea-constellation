## T-6.1 · Teacher Background

### Claude's web search — what it is, what it isn't

Claude's web search capability (when enabled) allows Claude to access current information from the internet rather than relying solely on its training data. This is important because:

- Claude's training has a **knowledge cutoff** — it does not know events after a certain date. ⏱
- Real-time information (timetables, opening hours, current news) requires live search.
- When Claude searches, it typically cites the source (website URL or domain name).

**Is web search always available?**
No. ⏱ Web search availability depends on:
- Whether the user has a free or paid Claude account.
- The region (some features roll out gradually).
- Whether the feature is enabled in Claude's settings.

**If web search is not available:** Claude will answer from its training data and may give outdated information. It should signal this ("As of my knowledge cutoff…"). If it does not signal this, students cannot tell the difference — which is the source literacy lesson.

**The core lesson here is not the tool — it is the habit:**
1. Where did this information come from?
2. When was it last updated?
3. Can I verify it on the original source?

This habit (source literacy) is one of the most transferable digital skills in the curriculum.

## T-6.4 · Scripts

### Introducing source literacy

> "Today we are going to talk about something very important.
> Not just AI — but *all* information on the internet.
>
> When you read something — on Claude, on Google, in a newspaper —
> you should always ask three questions.
> I will write them on the board.
>
> [Write:]
> 1. Where did this come from?
> 2. When was this written?
> 3. Can I check this on the official website?
>
> These three questions make you a careful reader.
> They protect you from wrong information."

---

### Showing Claude's knowledge cutoff (if web search is unavailable)

> "I am going to ask Claude something about today's news.
> Watch what it says.
>
> [Type: 'What is one piece of news from Australia today?']
>
> Look at Claude's reply.
> It says it does not have access to today's news.
> Or it gives information from a while ago.
>
> This is important. Claude learned from books and websites up to a certain date.
> After that date, Claude does not know what happened.
> We call this the knowledge cutoff.
>
> For old information — history, grammar, how things work — Claude is reliable.
> For today's information — use the official website."

---

### When Claude does search and finds a source

> "Claude found some information and told us the source.
> It says: transportnsw.info.
> What do we do now?
>
> We open that website ourselves.
> We check the information Claude gave us.
>
> Does the website say the same thing? If yes — good.
> If no — use the website. Not Claude."

## T-6.5 · Differentiation

### Pre-A1
- Student listens to the class discussion. Does not independently search.
- Activity: student looks at a printed timetable. Points to the bus time they need.
- Teacher types one query into Claude together. Student watches.

### A1
- Student copies one real query from the board into Claude.
- Teacher helps them identify the source Claude gives.
- Student visits the source website (teacher navigates, student reads one line).

### A2
- Student chooses their own local information query.
- Student independently finds and checks the source.
- Student completes the reliability checklist.

### B1
- Student compares Claude's answer to the official source. Notes any differences.
- Student writes one sentence about why source literacy matters.
- Extension: student finds one piece of information Claude got wrong and explains the error.

## T-6.6 · Common Student Questions and Model Answers

**Q1: "Why should I use Claude if I can just use Google?"**
> "Good question. Google shows you a list of websites. You choose one to read.
> Claude reads websites for you and summarises the answer.
> Both are useful. For quick plain-English answers: Claude.
> For finding the official document: Google or the website directly.
> Often the best approach is both."

**Q2: "Can Claude look at my local council website?"**
> "Claude can search the internet, which includes council websites.
> But always check the council website directly for official information.
> Council websites have the most current and accurate local information."

**Q3: "What is .gov.au?"**
> "'.gov.au' means the website is run by the Australian government.
> These are official, reliable sources.
> Examples: centrelink.gov.au, health.gov.au, immi.homeaffairs.gov.au.
> When you see .gov.au, you can generally trust the information."

**Q4: "Claude said the bus runs at 7:05. But the real timetable says 7:00. What do I do?"**
> "Excellent detective work. Use the real timetable.
> Claude made a small error.
> For anything time-sensitive — transport, appointments, deadlines —
> always check the official source."

**Q5: "Is there a way to tell if Claude searched or just guessed?"**
> "Sometimes Claude says 'According to [website]…' or gives a URL.
> That usually means it searched.
> If Claude does not mention a source, it may be answering from its training.
> You can ask Claude directly: 'Where did you get this information?'"

## T-6.7 · Things That Will Go Wrong

| Problem | What to do |
|---------|------------|
| Claude web search is not enabled | Use the knowledge cutoff demonstration as the lesson. Teach source literacy through the limitation — this is equally valid and more dramatic. |
| Claude gives wrong transport information | Treat it as a deliberate teaching moment: "Claude got this wrong. Let us check the official website." |
| Official website is hard to navigate | Acknowledge: "Government websites can be complicated. Claude can help you understand what you find there — but you need to visit the website first." |
| Student asks about information in their home country | Redirect: "Claude can search for that. But for Australian information, we focus on Australian sources first." |
