## T-10.1 · Teacher Background

### AI ethics — the plain teacher version

**What is AI ethics?**
Ethics is the study of what is right and wrong. AI ethics asks:
Who benefits from AI? Who is harmed? Who decides how it is used?

**Key concepts for this module:**

**Bias:** AI systems learn from human-produced data. That data reflects human prejudices.
If a hiring AI was trained on decades of hiring decisions that favoured men in certain roles,
it may reproduce that bias automatically — and at scale. This is not a hypothetical;
it has happened in real hiring tools used by large companies.

**Hallucination:** AI confidently states false information. It does not know it is lying.
It is pattern-matching, not truth-checking. Students have now seen this throughout the course.
This module names it explicitly.

**Opacity:** Many AI systems cannot explain *why* they made a decision.
If an AI recommends someone be denied a loan, it may not be possible to audit the reason.
This matters for legal rights.

**Consent and surveillance:** AI is increasingly used in public spaces (facial recognition,
voice recording). Students should know they can ask whether AI is being used in a service.

**Australian privacy law:** ⏱
The *Privacy Act 1988* governs how organisations handle personal information.
The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) handles complaints.
The government is currently updating AI governance frameworks — check oaic.gov.au for current status.

**Pedagogical note:** This is a values-based, discussion-centred lesson.
There are no wrong opinions. The goal is to develop students' capacity to think,
not to arrive at a class consensus. Teacher neutrality is important — present multiple
perspectives without advocating for one position.

## T-10.2 · Before You Teach

- [ ] Read the four key concepts above (bias, hallucination, opacity, consent).
- [ ] Prepare to facilitate a discussion — not lead one. Your role is to draw out student perspectives.
- [ ] Print the ethics scenarios from S-10.7 (one per student or one per pair).
- [ ] Have oaic.gov.au bookmarked for the rights discussion.
- [ ] This lesson may surface strong feelings — about technology, about surveillance, about fairness. That is normal and healthy. Create space for it without letting it dominate.
- [ ] Have a plan if the discussion becomes politically or personally charged (see T-10.7).

## T-10.4 · Scripts

### Opening — course reflection

> "You have completed nine lessons about AI.
> You have learned how to open Claude, how to speak to it, how to check its answers,
> how to protect your privacy, and how to use it for real tasks.
>
> Today is different. Today we are not learning *how* to use AI.
> Today we are asking: *should* AI be used for certain things?
> And who should decide?
>
> There are no right or wrong answers in this lesson.
> Your experience and your thinking matter here."

---

### Facilitating the ethics scenarios (S-10.7)

> "I am going to give each pair a scenario. A situation.
> Read it together.
> Decide: is this ethical — right? Or unethical — wrong?
> Why?
>
> There may not be a clear answer. That is OK.
> What matters is your reasoning — *why* you think what you think.
>
> After five minutes, each pair will share their thinking with the class.
> I will not tell you if you are right or wrong.
> I will ask you to listen to each other."

---

### Responding to a strong opinion without taking sides

> "That is a very interesting point. Thank you.
> Does anyone see it differently?
> [Pause for responses.]
> So we have two different views.
> Both are thoughtful.
> In the real world, people with both views are working on AI policy right now.
> Your opinion counts."

---

### Closing the course

> "You have finished 'AI in Everyday English.'
> You came here not knowing what AI was.
> Now you know:
> What AI is and what it is not.
> How to use it safely.
> How to check its answers.
> How to protect your privacy.
> And how to think critically about AI's role in the world.
>
> That is not a small thing. That is digital citizenship.
> You are ready to use these tools in your life — carefully and confidently.
>
> Well done."

## T-10.5 · Differentiation

### Pre-A1 / A1
- Teacher reads each scenario aloud.
- Student responds with thumbs up (ethical) or thumbs down (unethical).
- Teacher writes student responses on the board without judgment.

### A2
- Student pairs discuss scenarios. Sentence frame: "I think this is [ethical/unethical] because…"
- Student writes one sentence about one scenario.

### B1
- Student writes a 3–6 sentence opinion paragraph (S-10.9).
- Student leads discussion for one scenario.
- Extension: student writes a letter to an AI company explaining one rule they think AI should follow.

## T-10.6 · Common Student Questions and Model Answers

**Q1: "Can AI take over jobs — will I lose my job?"**
> "This is a real question that many people are asking.
> Some jobs are changing because of AI.
> New jobs are also appearing.
> What we know is: people who know how to use AI are more valuable in the workplace.
> You are learning those skills right now."

**Q2: "Can the government use AI to watch us?"**
> "Governments around the world are exploring how to use AI.
> Australia has privacy laws that limit how your information can be used.
> The OAIC website explains your rights.
> If you have a specific concern, a community legal centre can give advice."

**Q3: "Is Claude watching me right now?"**
> "Claude processes the text you type into it.
> It cannot see your camera, your other apps, or your files unless you share them.
> Your conversations may be stored by Anthropic.
> You can read their privacy policy at anthropic.com."

**Q4: "Should children use AI?"**
> "That is a very important question for families and schools to think about.
> Most AI services have a minimum age — usually 13 or 18.
> As a parent, you can set rules for how and when your children use technology.
> This is part of being a responsible AI user."

**Q5: "What happens if AI makes a decision that hurts me?"**
> "In Australia, you have the right to ask for a human review of any important decision
> made about you — especially in employment, housing, or government services.
> If AI made a decision that you believe was unfair, contact the OAIC or a community legal service."

## T-10.7 · Things That Will Go Wrong

| Problem | What to do |
|---------|-----------|
| Discussion becomes politically charged | Redirect: "This is a very important topic in politics. Let us focus today on the ethics — right and wrong — not on political parties." |
| Student shares a personal experience of unfair treatment | Acknowledge: "Thank you for sharing that. That sounds very difficult. The OAIC and community legal services can help with situations like this." Do not attempt to resolve the issue in class. |
| Student is very anxious about AI surveillance | Provide concrete facts: "Here is what Claude can and cannot see. [Review S-7.9.] Here are your rights under the Privacy Act." Anxiety is reduced by specific information. |
| Students have nothing to say | Start with a concrete scenario. Ask: "What would you think if a bank used AI to decide whether to give you a loan?" Almost everyone has a view on this. |
| Student says AI is completely bad / should be banned | Validate: "That is a perspective many people have. What specific thing worries you most? Let us look at that." Keep it concrete. |

## T-10.11 · Extension for Fast Finishers

**"Letter to an AI company"**

Student writes a short letter (5–8 sentences) to an AI company.
The letter:
- Names one thing they want the company to do better.
- Explains why it matters to them personally.
- Asks one specific question about privacy.

Share letters with the class. Display on the classroom wall.
