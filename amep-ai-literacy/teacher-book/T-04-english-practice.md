## T-4.1 · Teacher Background

### Claude as a grammar feedback tool — what it can and can't do

Claude is a genuinely useful grammar checker for ESL learners, with important caveats:

**What Claude does well:**
- Identifies common grammatical errors (tense, subject-verb agreement, articles, prepositions).
- Explains *why* something is wrong in plain language.
- Provides examples and patterns ("after 'need,' use 'to' + verb").
- Adjusts the complexity of its explanation if asked ("explain in simple words").
- Generates multiple examples on request.

**What Claude does less well:**
- Register and tone advice (formal vs informal) can be inconsistent.
- Australian English variants may occasionally be corrected toward US English. ⏱
- It may over-correct or give prescriptive rules that native speakers routinely break.
- It cannot hear a student's spoken English — pronunciation and fluency are beyond its text-based feedback.

**Pedagogical note:** This module positions Claude as a *feedback loop*, not a replacement for teacher feedback. Students generate a sentence, Claude responds, and the student brings both to the teacher for discussion. This keeps the teacher central to the learning.

## T-4.4 · Scripts

### Normalising mistakes at the start of the lesson

> "Before we start, I want to say something important.
> Every person in this room makes grammar mistakes in English.
> I make mistakes in other languages.
> Mistakes are how we learn.
> Today we are going to use Claude to help us find our mistakes.
> This is not about getting it right the first time.
> It is about learning from what we write."

---

### Introducing the prompt structure

> "When we ask Claude to check our English, we use a special sentence.
> I will write it on the board.
> [Write: 'Please check my sentence: ']
> After the colon, you write your sentence.
> That is it. Claude will do the rest.
>
> Let me show you.
> I will type: 'Please check my sentence: I am very boring today.'
> Watch what Claude says."
>
> *(Claude will likely note that 'boring' should be 'bored.')*
>
> "Did you see that? Claude told me which word was wrong.
> It also told me *why* it is wrong.
> Now I understand — 'boring' means something is not interesting.
> 'Bored' means I feel that way. Very different."

---

### When Claude's correction is itself wrong

> "Sometimes Claude makes a mistake. That can happen.
> If Claude says something that does not sound right to you — trust your feeling.
> Write the question down. Ask me after class.
> I have experience that Claude does not have."

## T-4.5 · Differentiation

### Pre-A1
- Student dictates a simple sentence (3–4 words) to you. You type it into Claude together.
- Focus on: "Look — Claude found one word to change. Can you see which word?"

### A1
- Student writes one sentence. Teacher helps them form the prompt. They send it and read the first two lines of the reply.

### A2
- Student writes 2–3 sentences from their own life. They ask Claude to check all of them.
- Student identifies one grammar rule they learned from the correction.

### B1
- Student asks Claude for three different examples of one grammar pattern.
- Student writes their own original examples using the pattern.
- Extension: student writes a short paragraph and asks Claude to improve it — then compares the two versions.

## T-4.6 · Common Student Questions and Model Answers

**Q1: "Can I ask Claude to correct my homework?"**
> "Yes — Claude can help you improve your homework.
> But your homework should show *your* ideas and *your* work.
> Use Claude to check, not to write for you.
> We will talk about this more in Module 10 — AI and ethics."

**Q2: "Claude gave me a different answer from you. Who is right?"**
> "Good question. Language can have more than one correct form.
> Let us look at both answers together and see which fits better.
> Sometimes I will be right. Sometimes Claude will be right. Sometimes both work."

**Q3: "Does Claude know Australian English?"**
> "Usually yes. But it may sometimes use American English.
> For example: 'centre' vs 'center,' or 'recognise' vs 'recognize.'
> In Australia we use the British/Australian spelling.
> If Claude uses American spelling, just note it — it is not wrong, just different."

**Q4: "Can I ask Claude in my language?"**
> "Yes. Claude understands many languages.
> But for this activity, try to write your sentence in English first.
> Then ask Claude to check it. That is how you practise."

**Q5: "Claude told me my sentence is correct. But my teacher said it is wrong."**
> "Bring both answers to me. Let us look together.
> Claude is helpful but not always right.
> When Claude and your teacher disagree, come to me first."

## T-4.7 · Things That Will Go Wrong

| Problem | What to do |
|---------|-----------|
| Claude gives a very long grammar explanation | Say: "We only need to read the first correction. The rest is extra. Let us focus on one thing at a time." Ask Claude: "Please give a shorter explanation." |
| Student becomes anxious about grammar mistakes | Redirect: "I see Claude found one thing to change. That is great — one thing to learn. You are doing well." Never tally errors aloud. |
| Claude corrects something that is actually correct | Say: "Interesting. Let me check that. [Check with your own knowledge or resources.] Claude can be wrong. This is why we always double-check." |
| Student asks Claude to write their whole assignment | Pause class. Discuss: "What is the purpose of homework? Who learns when Claude does the writing?" Use this as a preview of Module 10. |

## T-4.11 · Extension for Fast Finishers

1. Student writes five sentences about their week. They ask Claude to check all five.
2. Student writes the "rule" they learned from each correction (e.g., "Past tense of 'go' is 'went'").
3. Student teaches one rule to the class: "I learned that…"
