# Who Jesus Really Is, According to the Urantia Book

This is the one that changes everything, if you let it.

I grew up with the standard pictures of Jesus: the gentle teacher of the Sermon on the Mount, the suffering servant of Good Friday, the risen Lord of Easter Sunday. Those pictures carry genuine truth. But the Urantia Book adds a dimension that none of the gospels could quite convey, and that dimension has never left me.

The book says that Jesus of Nazareth was, at the same time, a fully human man *and* the creator of our local universe, a being it calls Michael of Nebadon.

In the cosmology of the Urantia Book, the universe is organised into levels: the central Isle of Paradise at the centre, then superuniverses, then local universes, each one created and administered by a Creator Son: a being of immense spiritual authority who is a genuine child of the infinite God. Our local universe, called Nebadon, has about ten million inhabited worlds. Its Creator Son is Michael.

And Michael chose, in a decision of sovereign will, to come to *this* world, the most troubled, most backward of his inhabited spheres, and live a complete human life, from birth to death, not as a disguise but as a genuine human experience. He was hungry. He was tired. He had friends who frustrated him and days when the work felt thankless. He died on a Roman cross, not as a ritual sacrifice to appease divine wrath, but as the fullest possible demonstration that love can be taken to the limit and still remain love.

The Urantia Book calls this the bestowal of Michael. And because he came as a human, he understands human life from the inside, not as a God-figure watching from above, but as one who washed dusty feet, wept at a friend's grave, and asked, in his darkest hour, whether there was another way.

The resurrection is not explained away. The book treats it as a real event, the reappearance of the morontia form of Jesus, a form of existence that is neither purely physical nor purely spiritual but something in between, a glimpse of what awaits all of us beyond physical death.

What the book adds is *scale*: the same Jesus who told the parable of the prodigal son is also the sovereign of a universe of ten million worlds. His tenderness and his authority are the same thing, at different magnitudes.

I don't expect you to accept that on my word. But I'd ask you this: does it make Jesus smaller or larger in your imagination? To me, it made the Sermon on the Mount feel like cosmic policy, not the advice of a wise man, but the governing principle of an entire universe.

The full text is free at [urantia.org](https://www.urantia.org). Part IV (Papers 120–196) is the longest account of the life of Jesus ever written, day by day, including years the gospels skip entirely.
