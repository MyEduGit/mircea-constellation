# "Doing the Will of God", Without the Robes

There's a phrase that used to make me uneasy: *doing the will of God.* It sounded like surrender, like handing over your own judgement, becoming a person who waits to be told, who blames or credits heaven for everything and takes responsibility for nothing. For a long time it felt like the opposite of growing up.

The Urantia Book gave that phrase back to me, too, by saying something so plain it almost slips past you: doing the will of God doesn't mean doing anything religious at all. It means *wholeheartedly choosing to do what is right*, and then doing it, in whatever ordinary corner of life you happen to occupy.

That's the whole of it. No robes. No special vocabulary. No waiting for a sign in the clouds.

The book is insistent that the will of God isn't a secret plan you have to decode, a single hidden answer you'll be punished for missing. It's much closer to a *direction* than a destination, the simple, available choice, in this moment, to be honest rather than deceitful, kind rather than cruel, courageous rather than cowardly. You don't need a priest to tell you which is which. You usually already know. The difficulty was never knowing; it was choosing.

And here's the part that freed me. In this telling, the most spiritual thing a person can do isn't to retreat from the world into prayer and ceremony. It's to do *the next right thing* in the life you actually have, to be a good parent, an honest worker, a patient neighbour, a truthful friend. The cab driver who is genuinely kind to a frightened passenger is, in the book's view, doing the will of God as truly as any saint. Maybe more truly, because no one is watching and there's no reward in it but the goodness itself.

I find this enormously practical, and at my age, enormously consoling. It means I haven't missed my chance. I don't need a grand mission or a pulpit or years I no longer have. The arena is right here, this conversation, this small decision, this ordinary Tuesday. The will of God isn't somewhere else, waiting for a better, holier version of me to show up. It's the honest choice in front of me, now.

You don't have to believe in God to test this. The next time you face a small fork, the easy lie or the harder truth, the convenient unkindness or the inconvenient decency, just choose the higher one, on purpose, and notice what it does to you. The book says that's the most important thing a human being ever does. I've come to think it's right.

The full text is free at [urantia.org](https://www.urantia.org). This idea, that the divine will is found in ordinary, wholehearted right choosing, is, to me, the beating heart of the whole book.
