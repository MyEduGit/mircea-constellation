# When the Universe Got a Lot Bigger

When I was young, the universe I was handed felt strangely small. There was the Earth, there was heaven somewhere above it, and there was God — close by, watching. That was about it. The whole drama of existence fit in a space you could almost draw on a single page.

Then science came along and did the opposite. It made everything enormous — billions of galaxies, billions of years — but somehow also empty. Vast, yes, but cold. A lot of room, and no one home.

One of the first things that struck me about The Urantia Book is that it refuses both of those pictures. It gives you a universe every bit as immense as the one astronomers describe — and then it fills it.

The book describes reality as organised, layered, *inhabited.* Not a few special places surrounded by dead space, but level upon level of worlds, systems, and constellations, each with its own purpose and its own residents, all the way up. It describes a central, eternal core to creation, and around it the great sweep of evolving space — including, far out toward the edges, our own small, important world.

Now — I have to be honest with you here, the way I'd want someone to be honest with me. The book's specific astronomy doesn't always line up with what telescopes have measured since 1955, and you'll find readers who argue about exactly how its geography maps onto modern cosmology. I'm not going to pretend it's a science textbook. That's not what moves me about it.

What moves me is the *shape* of the claim: that the bigness of the universe is not a reason to feel small and pointless, but the opposite. The book's universe is large *because* it's purposeful — because there is somewhere to go, a long journey upward and inward that every surviving human being is invited onto. The scale isn't there to humble you into despair. It's there because the adventure is genuinely that big.

That reframing did something for me. The same night sky that can make you feel like a speck became, instead, a map of where the journey leads. Same stars. Completely different feeling.

You don't have to take the cosmology literally to feel the pull of that idea — that the universe is not an empty mansion but a crowded, working, purposeful one, and that you have a place in it.

The text is free at [urantia.org](https://www.urantia.org). The early papers sketch the architecture of it all; later ones bring it down to our own world.

— M. G. Matthews
