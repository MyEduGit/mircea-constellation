# What "Light and Life" Actually Means

I keep using a phrase in these letters — *light and life* — and I owe you an honest explanation of it, because it's the closest thing I have to a reason for writing any of this at all.

In The Urantia Book, "light and life" is the name for what a world becomes when it finally grows up. Not destroyed, not raptured away, not abandoned — but *matured.* It's the book's vision of a planet that has slowly, over long ages, learned to live the way it was always meant to: where war has burned itself out, where no one goes hungry while others hoard, where the pursuit of truth and beauty and goodness becomes the ordinary business of ordinary days. A world at peace with itself and consciously at work on becoming better.

It is, in plain terms, the book's word for *a planet that has come of age.*

Now, I'm 70 years old, and I want to be honest with you about why a phrase like that matters to someone my age, watching the news. It would be easy to read "light and life" as a fairy tale — a comforting story for people who can't bear how the world actually is. I understand that reaction. I've felt it.

But here is what the book insists on, and what I've come to believe: light and life is not something that *happens to* a world. It's something a world *builds*, one generation at a time, one changed person at a time, through ordinary acts of honesty and kindness and patience that no one applauds. It isn't a magic future that arrives. It's a direction you can start walking today, knowing you won't see the destination — and walking anyway, because the walking is the point.

That reframes everything. It means the long view and the small act are the same thing. The person who tells the truth when a lie is easier, who feeds someone, who teaches a child, who refuses to add to the world's cruelty — that person is not waiting for light and life. They're *building* it, in the only place anyone ever can: right where they're standing.

I won't live to see the world I'm describing. That used to bother me. It doesn't anymore. The teaching gave me a strange kind of peace about it: my job was never to finish the work. It was only to take it a little further and hand it on.

That's why I write these. Not to convert you to a book — to invite you to help build the thing the book points at, whatever you end up believing about the rest of it.

The full text is free at [urantia.org](https://www.urantia.org). The idea of light and life runs through the whole of it, but it shines brightest in the later papers, after the long story has been told.

Thank you for reading. There's more to come — and I'm glad you're here for it.

— M. G. Matthews
