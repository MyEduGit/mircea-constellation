# What Adam and Eve Were Really For

Few stories are more familiar, or more freighted, than Adam and Eve. The Urantia Book tells it again, and the version it tells is so different from the one I grew up with that it took me a long time to absorb.

In the book's account, Adam and Eve were real, but they were not the first humans. Humanity had already evolved over hundreds of thousands of years before they arrived. Adam and Eve were something else: a pair of beings from a higher order, called Material Sons and Daughters, sent to our world on a specific mission, to biologically and spiritually uplift the human races that already existed, gradually improving the inheritance of the whole species over many generations.

They were not sent to be worshipped. They were not sent to live in idle paradise. They were sent to *work*, to establish a centre of culture, learning, and improved life, and to patiently elevate the world over a long period of time.

And the "fall," in the book's telling, was not the eating of a forbidden fruit. It was something more human and more poignant: an act of impatience. Eve, persuaded that the mission was going too slowly, tried to shortcut the long, careful plan. The shortcut failed. The mission was compromised. Adam and Eve became, in the book's phrase, "defaulters", not damned, not cursed, but having forfeited their special status through a well-intentioned mistake.

What moves me about this version is how *recognisable* it is. The fall is not cosmic wickedness. It is the very human error of trying to rush something that could only be done slowly, of losing faith in the patient plan and reaching for the quick result. I have made that mistake more times than I can count.

And the book is clear that even the default was not the end. The work continued, in slower and harder ways. Grace persisted. The long project of planetary progress went on, wounded but not destroyed.

There is a lesson in that for anyone trying to do good in the world: the patient way is usually the right way, and the shortcut usually costs more than it saves.
