# The Courage to Choose the Higher Thing

I want to close this stretch of writing where the book itself always lands: not in the heavens, but here, in the small and difficult business of ordinary living.

The Urantia Book is full of cosmic grandeur — millions of worlds, orders of celestial beings, a journey to Paradise that spans aeons. But it never lets you forget that the whole vast adventure turns on something tiny and immediate: the next choice you make. The higher thing or the lower thing. Right now, today, in the situation actually in front of you.

The book has a phrase for the work of a human life: *doing the will of God.* And it takes pains to strip that phrase of its religious heaviness. Doing the will of God, it says, does not mean grand gestures, dramatic sacrifices, or retreating from the world. It means, simply and demandingly, choosing the higher path in the ordinary moments — being honest when a lie is easier, patient when you want to lash out, generous when you could hoard, kind when no one would notice if you weren't.

That is harder than any grand gesture, because it never stops. There is always a next choice. The cashier who shortchanged you. The relative who pushes every button. The temptation to cut the corner no one will see. The book says these unglamorous moments are the actual material of soul-building — that eternity is shaped not in cathedrals but in kitchens, not in great trials but in the thousand small decisions that no one will ever applaud.

And it promises this: you are not alone in the choosing. The fragment of God within you is always present, always leaning gently toward the higher thing, always ready the moment you turn toward it. You will not always choose well. No one does. But the direction of a life — the slow, cumulative drift toward the light or away from it — is set by which way you *lean*, choice after choice, day after day.

So that is what I would leave you with, after all these weeks of writing. Not a doctrine to believe. A practice to attempt: in the next ordinary moment that asks something of you, choose the higher thing. Then do it again. That, the book says, is the whole of the spiritual life — and it is enough.

Thank you for reading. I'll keep writing, and I'm glad you're here.

— M. G. Matthews
