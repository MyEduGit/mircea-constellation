# If God Is Good, Why Is the World Broken?

This is the question that empties the pews. More people have walked away from God over this one than over any argument in a book: if there's a loving God in charge, why is there so much suffering, so much cruelty, so much that is plainly *wrong*?

I won't insult you with an easy answer. There isn't one. But I'll tell you the answer The Urantia Book gives, because it's the most satisfying I've found — and it doesn't ask you to look away from the pain.

The book's answer turns on one word: *freedom.*

It says that God made beings genuinely free — free to choose good or evil, love or cruelty, truth or the lie. And freedom that can't be misused isn't freedom at all; it's a puppet show. A love that can't be refused isn't love. So the same gift that makes goodness *real* — the fact that you *chose* it — is the gift that makes evil possible. You can't have one without the other. A universe with real freedom in it is a universe where things can genuinely go wrong.

And the book says, plainly, that things *did* go wrong. It tells of a great rebellion — beings of high trust who chose themselves over the whole, and the long damage that followed, our own world among the wounded. It doesn't pretend the brokenness is an illusion or a misunderstanding. It says: yes, it's real, and here is how a free universe came to carry it.

But here's the part that matters, and the part that gave me peace. The book insists the brokenness is *temporary.* Evil is not a permanent fixture of reality, equal and opposite to good. It's a wrong turn inside a story that is still being written — and the story bends, slowly, undefeated, toward healing. Nothing chosen against the good gets the last word. The repair is patient, it works through changed people rather than thunderbolts, and it does not fail.

That reframed my anger at the world's suffering. The pain is real — the book never tells me to stop grieving it. But it tells me the pain is not *proof of God's absence.* It's the cost of a universe where love and goodness can be freely chosen — and it's being answered, mended, outlasted, by a patience longer than history.

You can disagree with all of it. But if "why is the world broken?" has ever cost you your faith, or kept you from having any, I think this answer is worth your time. It looks the suffering in the eye and still finds a reason to hope.

The full text is free at [urantia.org](https://www.urantia.org). The papers on freedom, rebellion, and the long repair are difficult and worth it.
